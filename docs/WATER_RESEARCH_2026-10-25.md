# NSW water systems: research synthesis and modelling evidence

**Research synthesis — 25 October 2026**  
**Geography:** New South Wales (NSW), Australia; named utility service areas, catchments and Murray–Darling Basin geography are distinguished where relevant.  
**Status:** Desk-research synthesis of six successful research notes and the cited public sources. Not an operational status report, engineering design, asset register, or determination of drinking-water safety or reuse approval.  
**Evidence discipline:** Numbered citations refer to the source register at the end. Statements are presented as source-reported facts unless explicitly marked **Inference**, **Recommendation**, or **Unverified**. Live and annual figures retain their dates and stated scope.

## Scope and status

This report synthesizes evidence on NSW water-resource governance, source availability, bulk and utility supply, drinking-water quality, wastewater and reuse, selected regional town systems, public data and modelling limits. It is intended to guide repository research and careful modelling, not to represent all NSW infrastructure. The six underlying research notes cover governance, regional/town drinking water, wastewater and recycling, hydrology, regional case studies, and data/GIS evidence.

The research establishes important institutional roles and selected source-to-customer or wastewater examples. It does **not** establish a complete current inventory of water or sewer assets, street-level network topology, asset condition, hydraulic connectivity, complete service boundaries, or permission to reuse any particular dataset. Current operational values and project statuses must be re-checked at the cited publisher before they are used operationally.

## System overview

NSW water is best understood as several linked but non-identical systems: water-resource planning and allocation; catchments, rivers, groundwater and storage; bulk-water operations and transfers; drinking-water and wastewater utilities; public-health oversight; stormwater; recycled water; and, within the Murray–Darling Basin, intergovernmental basin governance. Water-sharing plans under the *Water Management Act 2000* are legally binding and establish source-specific resource rules; they are not utility pipe-network maps. [1]

WaterNSW describes its role in managing dams and raw-water supply infrastructure. Sydney Water, Hunter Water and regional local water utilities have distinct service and geographic roles downstream; no one utility description should be generalized statewide. [2][3][4][5] NSW DCCEEW reports 92 local water utilities serving around 2 million people in its strategy context, while its FY2024–25 performance dashboard covers 93 regional utilities and records reports from 80. These counts use different source contexts and should not be reconciled as though they described the same population or reporting universe. [5][6]

A useful conceptual chain is **catchments/aquifers → resource plans, entitlements and allocations → storage, river operations and bulk transfer → utility intake and treatment → distribution/customer use → sewer collection and wastewater treatment → recycling and/or environmental release**. Stormwater capture and drainage form related but distinct pathways; in some places runoff is harvested as a source, while drainage and wastewater collection are not interchangeable systems. This is a conceptual synthesis, not an authoritative statewide network diagram. [1][2][3][7]

NSW includes coastal catchments and catchments within the Murray–Darling Basin. The Basin Plan and River Murray operations add a basin/interstate layer for relevant geography; they do not govern every NSW coastal system. Catchment, water source and plan boundaries therefore matter when joining data or comparing availability. [8][9]

## Supply and treatment chain

### Resource, storage and bulk supply

Water availability is source- and catchment-specific. NSW DCCEEW manages water-sharing plans and allocations, including groundwater management; the relevant plan, source and entitlement category must be consulted rather than inferred from a statewide storage statistic. [1][10] WaterNSW's dam and infrastructure descriptions establish operator and asset-class context, but do not certify a complete public asset register. [2][11]

WaterNSW's Regional NSW Dam Levels page is updated daily and explicitly says its information is not exhaustive. On the snapshot retrieved 25 October 2026 it reported 63% regional storage; listed examples included Menindee Lakes at 26%, Burrendong at 33%, Lostock at 99%, and Toonumbar at 102%. These are reported storage values, not water allocation, deliverable supply, treatment capacity, or a local utility's security measure. Preserve the retrieval date and the source's reported percentage, including values above 100%, rather than silently normalizing them. [12]

Drought is not a single interchangeable metric. The Bureau of Meteorology (BoM) drought viewer presents distinct rainfall, soil-moisture, evaporative-stress, streamflow, storage and groundwater indicators with different periods, baselines and reporting frequencies. A catchment indicator may inform context but does not by itself measure a town's remaining supply, entitlement, treatment capacity or customer exposure. [13]

### Urban and regional utility supply

Sydney Water reports serving more than 5 million people across Greater Sydney, the Blue Mountains and Illawarra; supplying about 1.5 billion litres/day of drinking water; and sourcing about 80% of its water from Lake Burragorang behind Warragamba Dam, with Shoalhaven and desalination pathways available when supply declines. It also reports about 100 million litres/day of recycled water. These are operator-reported web-page figures; the page does not specify a measurement period for the daily rates. [3]

Regional systems are heterogeneous. The FY2024–25 DCCEEW dashboard reports 83% surface water among self-sourced water, 49 utilities treating effluent for recycled-water reuse, and a median unplanned water-supply interruption duration of 120 minutes. The dashboard says 80 of 93 regional utilities reported and that the indicator set changed in 2024–25; statistics are sector descriptors, not a full census, town-specific risk score, or directly comparable time series without definition and participation checks. [6]

DCCEEW strategic guidance directs utilities to assess source access and reliability against supply/demand change, climate risk, population growth and extreme events. Source types can include surface water, groundwater, recycled water, stormwater, rainwater, seawater and imports; rainfall-independence is one planning lens, not a guarantee of reliability or suitability. [14]

### Drinking-water safety and health oversight

NSW Health endorses the Australian Drinking Water Guidelines (ADWG) and a catchment-to-tap multiple-barrier approach. NSW suppliers, including utilities, private suppliers and water carters, are subject to quality-assurance / drinking-water management system requirements under the Public Health Act 2010 and Public Health Regulation 2022, as described by NSW Health. The framework includes risk controls such as filtration and disinfection; suppliers review implementation annually, and NSW Health recommends a full stakeholder review every four years. [15]

Regional supplies are monitored for microbiological and chemical quality through NSW Health's Drinking Water Monitoring Program, with scope and frequency determined by risk and program requirements. The existence of a monitoring program does not demonstrate that every contaminant is measured at every supply at the same frequency. NSW Health assigns PFAS risk assessment to utilities and recommends catchment-informed testing plans; this is not evidence that all supplies completed testing or that a particular supply has a detection. Some small-town supplies may be non-potable, untreated or not monitored to ADWG expectations; do not assume every town supply is treated potable water. [15][16]

WaterNSW's Town Water Risk Reduction Program (TWRRP) phase-two reporting describes support to 14 local water utilities and monthly sampling at key locations for up to 15 parameters. This is program-specific activity, not statewide monitoring coverage or routine testing scope for every utility. [17]

## Wastewater, stormwater and reuse

DCCEEW reports 82.8 GL of recycled-water production in 2023–24 by NSW public, local and private utilities. Production is not equivalent to volume delivered to end users or beneficially reused. DCCEEW's annual local-utility performance database reports supply and sewerage performance; it is not a complete geospatial asset inventory. [18][5]

Sydney Water reports collecting and treating about 1.5 billion litres/day of wastewater before reuse or environmental release. Its FY2024–25 environmental report gives **modelled wet-weather overflow** totals of 212 ML from 14 inland wastewater systems and 7,629 ML from eight ocean systems, lower than the previous year's reported modelled totals. These are modelled volumes, not metered releases, and the report contextualizes the year-on-year difference partly by rainfall and storm activity. The figures apply to Sydney Water's reported systems, not NSW statewide. [3][19]

Hunter Water describes 19 wastewater treatment plants and reuse for open space irrigation, industry, agriculture and residential uses. It reports more than 1,100 homes connected to residential schemes in Chisholm and Gillieston Heights since 2019 and around 45,000 wet tonnes of biosolids produced annually and beneficially reused. Wet tonnes include water content; utility claims and estimates retain the source's scope and are not statewide totals. [20]

Stormwater roles, funding and coordination can involve councils and catchment-scale bodies, but these facts do not establish universal asset ownership or boundary alignment. Sydney Water describes stormwater services in parts of Greater Sydney and works with other agencies elsewhere; the Orange case demonstrates stormwater harvesting as a drought diversification measure. Drainage, sewerage, potable-water and recycled-water layers should remain separate in any model. [3][21][22]

The Australian Guidelines for Water Recycling provide risk-management guidance; they do not themselves grant permission for a particular site or end use. Site-specific reuse depends on approvals, operating controls, quality and permitted purpose. No reuse permission should be inferred from a research note, dataset, or example project. [23]

## Regional town case-study recommendations

These are recommended **research/model validation cases**, not claims of current operational status or model readiness. The rationale is that published evidence describes contrasting and legible system patterns; each remains incomplete without direct utility verification.

| Recommended case | Evidence basis and research value | Explicit caveats / validation required |
|---|---|---|
| **Broken Hill** | DCCEEW identifies the Wentworth–Broken Hill pipeline as 270 km; a NSW connectivity-panel report says Broken Hill has received Murray River water via pipeline since 2019. It is a useful long-distance bulk-source-transfer case, with Menindee Lakes as important historical/basin context. [24][25] | These sources do not establish local distribution topology, current pipeline condition/capacity, operating performance, present source mix, or complete asset coverage. Verify current operations and status with responsible operators before modelling. |
| **Cobar–Nyngan** | A July 2024 DCCEEW Review of Environmental Factors describes raw water from the Bogan River weir pool at Nyngan transferred through approximately 130 km of dual pipelines serving Hermidale, Cobar, mines and smaller consumers, plus an approximately 10 km dual transfer link to the treatment plant. This provides an unusually specific bulk-system case. [26] | The REF documents project scope and conditions at publication; Stage 1 pump-station works were funded while Stage 2 pipeline replacement awaited an investment decision. Proposed works must not be treated as completed. Verify current staging, assets, service demand, routing and operational status. The REF is not a complete GIS. |
| **Orange stormwater harvesting** | A CRC for Water Sensitive Cities case study reports the historical drought context (combined storage 26.7% in August 2008; restrictions from 2003 reduced use around 38%, to below 4,390 ML average annual use in 2008) and describes Blackmans Swamp Creek stormwater harvesting and a dual-water approach in new developments. Useful for source-diversification and potable/non-potable conceptual layers. [27] | Historical figures are not current conditions or current scheme throughput. The case study does not prove city-wide dual-pipe coverage, street-level topology, current operating rules, water quality or permission for a new reuse application. Seek current council/utility documentation and approvals. |

**Recommendation:** Begin comparative validation with these three cases because they represent (a) a long-distance regional source connection, (b) a documented multi-user raw-water transfer and treatment chain, and (c) stormwater harvesting/source diversification. Compare them by source, raw-water transfer, treatment, customer/service context, wastewater/reuse and data confidence; do not assume equivalent ownership, engineering design or service-area boundaries. [24][26][27]

A broader comparative frame may include Greater Sydney and Lower Hunter as separately documented urban utility contexts, but do not treat either as representative of all regional systems. [3][20]

## Datasets and asset-geography evidence

Public information is distributed across different products and purposes. NSW DCCEEW describes a water-data discovery space covering 760 water sources. WaterNSW reports more than 1,300 monitoring stations with surface-water, groundwater, storage, weather and sample observations. Counts describe published resource/monitoring coverage, not utility asset counts, uniform station density, or complete time series. [28][29]

WaterNSW warns that delivered near-real-time logger data may have little or no validation despite accredited collection activities. Its API attribution page requires attribution and disclaims warranty of suitability. Record retrieval time, validation status, units, station metadata and attribution; keep provisional telemetry distinct from quality-controlled history. [30][31]

BoM offers distinct products including Water Data Online, Geofabric, groundwater information, hydrologic reference stations, storage information and accounts/forecasts. NSW Hydro Line spatial data describes natural watercourses and waterbodies, not drinking-water or sewer pipes. Sydney Water's public-facing diagrams describe zones and larger mains, not a stated complete public pipe-connectivity dataset. DCCEEW's performance dashboard is utility reporting, not a geospatial network map. [32][33][34][6]

For each dataset, record publisher, exact dataset/API identifier and URL, retrieval timestamp, geographic and temporal coverage, coordinate reference system, units/datum, schema, validation status, transformations, licence, attribution, update cadence, access restrictions and any security/privacy limitations. Preserve original values and units. Verify metadata and terms for every dataset individually; public accessibility does not establish public-domain status, completeness, or permission to republish. [30][31][34]

**Asset-geography evidence conclusion:** The material reviewed supports natural hydrology, observations, planning/allocation information, selected utility service context and named project descriptions. It does not establish complete statewide street-level pipe topology, all asset locations, asset status, ownership at each interface, pressure zones, service connections, pipe condition, hydraulic connectivity or comprehensive wastewater/stormwater/recycled-water inventories. Do not infer engineered network edges from hydrography, generalized system diagrams or proximity. [2][6][26][33][34]

## Modelling layers versus real engineering and safety

A repository model can organize evidence without pretending to be an engineering model. Keep separate layers for: (1) natural features and catchments; (2) water-resource plans, entitlements and allocations; (3) observations and storage; (4) bulk infrastructure and transfers; (5) utility/service-area context; (6) drinking-water treatment and quality controls; (7) potable distribution assets, only where verified; (8) sewer collection and treatment; (9) recycled-water production/distribution/end use; and (10) stormwater drainage/harvesting. Attach provenance, geometry precision and confidence to each claim.

Use explicit labels **observed**, **provisional**, **modelled**, **forecast**, **historical**, and **planned/proposed**. Do not merge them without preserving source and time basis. Hydrologic modelling is conditional on model purpose/version, forcing data, calibration and independent validation periods, assumptions, spatial units and uncertainty. DCCEEW modelling resources support water-planning work; a public landing page is not a site-specific engineering design. [13][35]

A hydraulic or water-quality engineering model requires verified asset geometry and connectivity, elevations/levels and datum, dimensions/materials, operational states, demand and inflow, storage/valve/pump controls, treatment process data, calibration observations, boundary conditions and applicable operating rules. These inputs were not established comprehensively by this research. Any prototype should be described as a conceptual or research model unless validated against responsible-operator data and reviewed by qualified professionals.

Safety must not be inferred from source-water conditions or a map. Tap-water safety depends on treatment barriers, monitoring, risk management and incident response; water-resource chemistry or river observations alone do not establish customer water quality. Likewise, a recycled-water guideline is not a site-specific approval. Do not use this report to make operational decisions, certify potable status, determine environmental releases, or authorize reuse. [15][16][23]

## Evidence distinctions and unresolved verification gaps

**Verified/source-reported:** The cited agencies/operators publish the governance roles, counts, reported figures, guidance, program descriptions and case-study details summarized above. “Verified” here means supported by the cited source, not independently audited or field-checked.

**Inferences:** NSW water is a distributed, layered system rather than one statewide utility network; source/catchment boundaries and utility reporting scope need explicit treatment in comparative analyses; and the selected town cases illustrate distinct system patterns. These are reasoned interpretations of cited sources, not official statewide classifications. [1][2][5][8]

**Unresolved verification gaps:**

- Current, complete, georeferenced inventories for drinking-water, sewer, stormwater and recycled-water assets, including topology, status, ownership, condition, capacity, connectivity and service boundaries.
- Dataset-by-dataset schema, coordinate reference system, licence, attribution, redistribution permissions, sensitivity/security restrictions and completeness; no comprehensive terms audit or API test is asserted.
- Current town-specific source portfolios, yields, storage-days, restrictions, drought triggers, transfer/treatment capacity, customer exposure, monitoring results and incident history.
- Current status and performance of the Broken Hill pipeline, Cobar project stages and Orange harvesting/dual-water schemes; cited evidence is date-bound and does not establish present completion or operation.
- Harmonized definition and coverage for regional utility counts and performance indicators, including missing submissions and year-to-year comparability after the FY2024–25 redesign.
- Whether each supply is potable and which contaminants are monitored at what frequency; no statewide compliance calculation or PFAS testing-completion claim is made.
- Actual volumes of recycled water beneficially reused versus produced, site-specific approvals, quality controls and end-use permissions.
- Engineering calibration/validation evidence sufficient for a hydraulic, water-quality, asset-condition or operational model.
